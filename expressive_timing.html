Abstract

This paper introduces a novel approach for modeling expressive timing performance by combining cognitive, symbolic and graphic representations of rhythm spaces with Lindenmayer systems, originally conceived to model the evolution of biological cell structures and plant growth. Logo-turtle abstractions are proposed in order to generate expressive rhythmic performances defined by rule-based displacements through perceptual rhythm categories.

§ 1

Introduction — Expressiveness as Perceptual Deviation

In music performance, aspects such as rhythm, pitch, loudness, timbre and memory all contribute to our perception of expressiveness. Yet the expressive parameters employed vary considerably from one performer to another, even within the same piece — a common cause of disagreement when listeners compare judgements of expressiveness. Can we find an intrinsic definition of expressiveness, without reference to an external score? Are there perceptual constraints on expressiveness, and if so, can they be used to model performance?

Within the abundant literature on music performance modelling, different approaches define expressiveness in quite different ways. Davies defines it as the emotional qualities of music perceived by the listener; London identifies the amount of expressiveness the listener expects; Clarke approaches it through deviations from notated score durations; and Desain & Honing define expression intrinsically, in terms of a performance and its structural description — independent of a score.

"For the purpose of the current study we will define expressiveness as the deviation from the most frequently heard version of a constituent musical element."

Previous research shows that even when listeners require no explicit training to perceive expressive timing, memory and expectation play a fundamental role in recognising nuances in music timing. We can therefore hypothesise that the range of expectations and uncertainty in music will be partially determined by previous exposure to it.

Key Hypothesis

If expressive timing can be characterised as structured deviation within perceptual rhythm categories — rather than deviation from an abstract, integer-ratio metronomic norm — then we can use those empirically-derived categories as a generative space for expressive performance.

An illuminating example concerns ritardandi. According to Honing (2006), performers use non-linear models to convey expressiveness through different sorts of slowing-down. Non-linearity allows a player to perform the same piece differently each time, rather than repeating the same expressive formula. These non-linear models act as a communicative resource referring to a listener's memory and expectations — while also producing slight deviations that add novelty.

As an approach to modelling expressive performances within different rhythm patterns and mental representations, this paper proposes combining symbolic and graphic representations of rhythm spaces with Lindenmayer systems (L-systems) and logo-turtle abstractions. The resulting model can be used as an exploratory tool of expressive timing for computational creativity in music generation.

§ 2

Rhythm Categories and Expressive Timing

In the domain of rhythm, expressive timing is defined by the deviations — or nuances — that a performer may introduce in contrast to a metronomic interpretation. The ability of listeners to distil a discrete, symbolic rhythmic pattern from a series of continuous intervals requires understanding how the perception of rhythm occurs.

Rhythmic perceptual categories can be understood as mental clusters by which listeners relate expressive timing to a rhythmic pattern after having detected it — for example, the rhythmic pattern that would be symbolically transcribed in music dictation. Different interpretations of the same symbolic representation are possible depending on the performer's aesthetics, experience, and motor skills.

The Desain & Honing (2003) Study

Desain & Honing conducted an empirical study using a large set of temporal patterns as stimuli presented to musically trained participants. Through an identification task, rhythmic categories were collected by asking participants to notate in music notation what they heard — to guess what would be written in the score for a drummer playing that sequence. Repeating this process across every possible combination of four-onset rhythms of one-second duration, they sampled the entire perceived rhythm space.

The Chronotopological Map

The results can be displayed as a chronotopological map: a triangular space in which each point represents a rhythm of four onsets (three inter-onset intervals I₁, I₂, I₃ summing to 1 second). Each side of the triangle represents one inter-onset interval. The black dots mark the modal points — points of greatest agreement among participants, where entropy H = 0. Category boundaries represent H = 1.

Figure 1. Schematic chronotopological map showing rhythm categories (a–h visible here) as elliptical regions in the triangular inter-onset interval space. Each vertex corresponds to one interval. Modal centroids (•) and metronomic positions (×) differ — the most commonly perceived rendition of a rhythm is not integer-related.

A striking finding: the most frequently identified pattern (modal) is not aligned with the metronomical interpretation of the same rhythmic pattern. This suggests that deviations within a category do not confirm Clarke's definition of timing as deviations from integer-related durations as notated in a score. Instead, the most commonly perceived rendition of a rhythm contains a timing pattern — a slight speeding up and slowing down — that seems a more appropriate reference than the metronomical version, which might in fact be perceived as expressive.

These results also explain why traditional software tools treating expressive timing as a rounding-off algorithm are limited in expression and easily identified as machine-generated. The factors of tempo (40, 60, or 90 BPM), meter (duple, triple), and dynamic accent all affect the graphical representation of the rhythmic categories — varying the shape and size of each region.

§ 3

Lindenmayer Systems

Finding a relation between formal grammars and music syntax has been researched since the General Theory of Tonal Music (Lerdahl & Jackendoff, 1983), a theory inspired by Chomsky's formalization of language. In 1968, Lindenmayer proposed a similar mathematical formalism for modelling cell development and plant growth, in which a structure represented by symbols within a defined alphabet develops over time via string-rewriting.

Grammar Definition

A generative grammar \(G\) is defined by the 4-tuple:

\[ G = (N, \, S, \, \omega, \, p) \]

where \(N\) is a finite set of non-terminal symbols (variables), \(S\) is a set of terminal symbols (constants) disjoint from \(N\), \(\omega\) is the initial axiom string, and \(p\) is a set of production rules defining how variables are replaced. The essential difference between Chomsky grammars and L-systems is that in each L-system derivation, all symbols are replaced simultaneously rather than sequentially.

Three Types of Rules in Musical L-Systems

Rule Types (after Manousakis, 2006)

Production rules — each symbol is replaced by one or more symbols, determining the structural development of the model. The richness and variety of output depends on these.

Decomposition rules — 'unwrap' a compound symbol into sub-structures. Always context-free and effectively Chomsky productions.

Interpretation rules — after each derivation, translate the string output to the desired field and parameter. The expressive generative model focuses here; their mapping allows versatility while retaining simplicity.

Logo-Turtle Graphics

Prusinkiewicz extended L-systems with a graphical interpretation using a logo-style turtle. The turtle's state in 2D is a triplet \((x, y, \alpha)\) — Cartesian coordinates and heading angle. Given step size \(d\) and angle \(\delta\):

F → move forward, draw line from (x,y) to (x',y') where x' = x + d·cos α, y' = y + d·sin α f → move forward without drawing + → turn left by δ → new heading: (x, y, α + δ) - → turn right by δ → new heading: (x, y, α − δ)

This graphical metaphor is the key to the paper's novel contribution: mapping the turtle's trajectory not through abstract 2D space, but through the perceptual rhythm category space of the chronotopological map.

§ 4

Using L-Systems to Generate Expressiveness

In 1986, Prusinkiewicz proposed a musical application of L-systems. Since then, several musical approaches have been proposed — composing music, generating real-time evolving audio synthesis, parsing music structure from scores. However, L-systems had not previously been combined with perceptual constraints.

The key advantage of incorporating L-systems into a perceptual model of expressiveness is that since the semantic relation to the modelled structure is symbolic, there is "no topological similarity or contiguity between the sign and the signifier, but only a conventional arbitrary link" (Manousakis, 2006). This versatility — combined with parallel symbol development — allows simultaneous attention to several perceptual categories and parameters (timing, loudness, timbre).

Implementation Strategy

The implementation uses the rhythmic categories as a conceptual space through which a logo-turtle moves to generate expressive timing within a musical bar of four onsets, according to prediction rules defined by the L-system. Three approaches are combined:

Geometrical Approximation

The complex geometrical shapes of each perceptual category are approximated by ellipses, providing a tractable mathematical representation of the category boundary. Category dimensions and orientations are derived from measurements of the empirical data (Desain & Honing, 2003).

Object Mapping

Each rhythmic category is represented by a letter in the L-system dictionary, allowing simultaneous use of different production rules for different expressive aspects (rhythm, loudness, timbre). Production rules alternate between rhythm categories, generating different rhythmic patterns.

Movement Mapping (Relative Spatial Mapping)

A logo-turtle maps its trajectory through the 2D perceptual space within a category's elliptical approximation. The step distance, controlled by the L-system, determines the degree of expressive deviation. The turtle's distance from the modal centroid corresponds to entropy H — proximity to the boundary means H → 1 (greater uncertainty / expressiveness).

How to Use the Demo

Controls

Presets — load a pre-configured L-system (Fibonacci, Dragon, Koch, Cantor). Each produces a different turtle path through rhythm space.

Axiom & Rules — define your own L-system. Use letters a–l for rhythm categories, and → to write rules (one per line). Any letter that maps to a rhythm category (a–l) will generate a timing event; other letters are structural.

Derivations — how many times to apply the production rules. More derivations = longer, more complex sequences.

Speed — the interval between animation steps in milliseconds. Lower = faster playback.

Sound — each step plays 4 timed beeps spaced by the actual I₁:I₂:I₃ ratios of the current category. Beat 1 is accented. Choose waveform (sine / triangle / square) and volume.

Triangle map — hover over categories to highlight them. The teal path shows the turtle's visited trajectory; the dim dashed line shows the full planned path. The pulsing dot is the current position.

§ 5

Summary and Conclusions

Despite much research in the field of music expressiveness generation, little attention has been paid to the possibility of using data from perceptual experiments to generate expressiveness. In order to embrace the necessary versatility to produce expressiveness in music, this paper presented a novel approach to modelling expressive timing performance by combining cognitive, symbolic, and graphic representations of rhythm spaces with Lindenmayer systems.

"The initial steps done on this expressive cognitive model seem promising to develop automatic music performance systems as well as to understand the cognitive aspects involved in expressiveness perception and generation of music."

Several open challenges remain. First, whether tempo and perceptual categories can be scaled proportionally by keeping a centroid relation derived from a morphological inference between categories — Sadakata et al. (2006) suggest that while score intervals scale proportionally with global tempo, the deviation of performed onsets with respect to the score is invariant of that. Second, how to correlate turtle positions across different rhythmic categories, so that expressive deviations in one bar are consistent with those in the next.

Solving these challenges would allow the turtle to jump in a continuous music line from one category to another — "mirroring" positions — while remaining coherent with the degree of expressiveness across them. Using entropy as a measure to compare categories and estimate performance complexity before the boundary of a category is reached seems an optimal solution, following the Bayesian framework of Sadakata et al.

Nevertheless, it remains a challenge to scale from a microworld approach (as presented here) to a more realistic model of expressive performance, and all of the proposals still await proper evaluation, validation and empirical support.
